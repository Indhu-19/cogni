# Personal Finance & Budgeting Principles

## The 50/30/20 Rule
A budgeting framework that splits after-tax income into three buckets:
- 50% on Needs: rent, groceries, utilities, minimum debt payments, transport.
- 30% on Wants: dining out, entertainment, subscriptions, hobbies.
- 20% on Savings & Debt Repayment: emergency fund, investments, extra loan payments.
This rule is a starting heuristic, not a strict law — adjust ratios based on cost of living and income level.

## Emergency Fund
An emergency fund should cover 3–6 months of essential living expenses (rent, food,
utilities, minimum debt payments). It should be kept in a liquid, low-risk account
(savings account or liquid mutual fund) rather than invested in volatile assets,
since the point is instant access during a job loss, medical emergency, or major
unplanned expense.

## Needs vs Wants
A "Need" is an expense required to maintain basic living and work functionality:
rent, groceries, utility bills, transport to work/college, minimum insurance.
A "Want" is a discretionary expense that improves quality of life but isn't
essential: dining out, streaming subscriptions, shopping, entertainment, travel
for leisure. Categorizing spending into needs vs wants is the first step to
identifying where a budget can be trimmed.

## Subscription Creep
Subscription creep is the gradual accumulation of small recurring charges
(streaming, apps, cloud storage, gym memberships) that individually seem
negligible but collectively consume a significant portion of monthly spending.
A common recommendation is to audit all recurring subscriptions every quarter
and cancel unused ones.

## Debt Repayment Strategies
- **Avalanche method**: pay off debts in order of highest interest rate first,
  while making minimum payments on the rest. Minimizes total interest paid.
- **Snowball method**: pay off debts in order of smallest balance first.
  Builds psychological momentum through quick wins, even if it costs slightly
  more interest overall.

## Student Budgeting Tips
For students with limited or irregular income (stipends, part-time work,
allowance), it's recommended to:
1. Track every expense for at least one month before creating a budget.
2. Prioritize an emergency buffer, even if small, before discretionary spending.
3. Batch recurring expenses (subscriptions, memberships) and review them monthly.
4. Use the needs/wants split even on a small income, scaled proportionally.

## Categorization of Common Expenses
- Food: groceries, dining out, food delivery apps.
- Rent/Housing: rent, hostel fees, maintenance charges.
- Transport: fuel, cab/auto fares, public transit passes.
- Subscriptions: streaming services, cloud storage, app subscriptions, gym.
- Utilities: electricity, water, internet, mobile recharge.
- Entertainment: movies, events, gaming, hobbies.
- Savings/Investment: recurring deposits, mutual fund SIPs, stocks.

## SIP (Systematic Investment Plan) Basics
A SIP is a method of investing a fixed amount into a mutual fund at regular
intervals (usually monthly) rather than investing a lump sum at once. Key
benefits:
- **Rupee cost averaging**: since the fixed amount buys more units when
  prices are low and fewer units when prices are high, it smooths out the
  average purchase cost over time instead of timing the market.
- **Compounding**: returns generated are reinvested, so growth accelerates
  the longer the SIP continues — starting early matters more than the
  amount invested per month.
- **Discipline**: automating a fixed monthly investment builds a saving
  habit without requiring active decisions each month.
SIPs are commonly used for long-term goals (5+ years) since equity mutual
funds are volatile in the short term but tend to average out over longer
horizons. A SIP is not a separate asset class — it is simply a way of
investing into whatever mutual fund category (equity, debt, hybrid) is
chosen.

## Credit Score Basics
A credit score (in India, commonly a CIBIL score ranging 300–900) reflects
how reliably a person has repaid past debt. It affects loan/credit card
approval and the interest rate offered. Key factors that influence it:
- **Payment history**: paying credit card bills and loan EMIs on time is
  the single biggest factor. Missed or late payments hurt the score
  significantly.
- **Credit utilization**: using a high percentage of available credit
  limit (e.g. maxing out a credit card) can lower the score even if bills
  are paid on time. Keeping utilization below ~30% is a common guideline.
- **Length of credit history**: older, well-managed accounts help the
  score; closing old accounts can shorten average history length.
- **Credit mix**: a mix of secured (e.g. loans) and unsecured (e.g. credit
  cards) credit, managed responsibly, is viewed favorably.
- **Hard inquiries**: applying for many loans/cards in a short period
  triggers multiple hard inquiries, which can temporarily lower the score.
Students with no credit history typically start with no score at all
("credit invisible") rather than a bad score — the first credit card or
loan (even a small one, responsibly repaid) is usually how a credit
history begins.
